Chapter 3: What Makes Medicines Dangerous?
Page 4 — Simple English Translation
1. X-rays during pregnancy
- Exposing a fetus to X-rays is considered an animal experiment.
- Humans are mammals, so similar risks may apply to them.
- Even taking one X-ray photograph of a fetus is questionable.
- Taking hundreds of X-rays of the chest is even more concerning.
- The amount of radiation a fetus receives inside the womb is difficult to determine.
2. Dangers of X-rays to the fetus
- X-rays can harm a developing baby.
- The most dangerous period is between the 2nd and 8th week of pregnancy.
- Doctors must carefully decide whether to use X-rays or perform an artificial delivery.
- According to the text, the risk of a baby being born with abnormalities after exposure to radiation may be much higher than normal.
- The author mentions a figure of 94%, but this is a claim in the book, not an established medical fact.
3. Medicines and birth defects
- New medicines should not be sold without proper safety testing.
- However, testing medicines on animals cannot guarantee that they are safe for humans.
- The author questions whether animal experiments can reliably predict birth defects in human babies.
- Some medicines may cause abnormalities in developing babies.
- Doctors cannot always predict which medicines will cause harm.
4. Pregnancy and miscarriage
- Pregnancy does not always continue successfully.
- The author mentions that some pregnancies end in miscarriage during the early stages.
- Some people may experience miscarriage without even knowing they were pregnant.
- The text suggests that the possibility of miscarriage should be considered when discussing pregnancy-related risks.
5. Medicines that cause miscarriage
- Certain medicines can make miscarriage or premature delivery more likely.
- In the past, some medicines were used to cause abortion.
- Pregnant women may experience constipation, but the author warns against casually taking laxatives.
- Strong laxatives may stimulate the uterus and could be dangerous during pregnancy.
6. Doctors, pharmacists, and medicines
- Pharmaceutical companies sometimes promote medicines as safe and effective.
- Doctors may trust the information supplied by these companies.
- The author criticizes doctors who prescribe medicines without carefully examining their safety.
- Doctors should understand the medicines they prescribe.
- They should explain the treatment and possible risks to patients.
- Patients should not be treated as opportunities to sell medicines.
7. Patients should know what they are taking
- Patients have a right to know the names of their medicines.
- Doctors should explain why each medicine is being prescribed.
- Patients should understand the possible risks and benefits.
- The author argues that patients should not blindly trust medicines simply because a doctor prescribed them.
8. Aspirin during pregnancy
- The author warns that pregnant women should avoid taking aspirin without medical guidance.
- The text claims that aspirin taken close to delivery may increase the risk of bleeding in newborn babies.

Main message of this page: The author argues that medicines and X-rays can be dangerous during pregnancy. Doctors should carefully consider possible risks, and patients should understand what medicines they are taking.
