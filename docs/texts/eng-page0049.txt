Chapter 13: Infectious Diseases During Pregnancy

1. Infections and fetal development
- The author explains that some infections during pregnancy can affect the developing baby.
- Certain viruses can pass from the mother to the fetus.
- These infections may cause birth defects or other complications.
- The author emphasizes that preventing infections during pregnancy is important.
2. Rubella infection during pregnancy
- Rubella, also known as German measles, is a viral infection.
- The author explains that rubella infection during early pregnancy can be dangerous.
- The virus may interfere with the development of the baby's organs.
- Possible abnormalities mentioned include problems affecting:
  - The eyes.
  - The ears.
  - The heart.
- The author warns that infection during the first few months is particularly concerning.
3. Rubella and congenital abnormalities
- The author explains that rubella infection can sometimes cause congenital rubella syndrome.
- This condition may affect several parts of the baby's body.
- The book states that infection during the first five months of pregnancy may carry risks.
- However, the risk is especially high during early pregnancy.
- The author emphasizes the importance of rubella prevention.
4. Rubella vaccination before pregnancy
- The author recommends checking rubella immunity before becoming pregnant.
- Women who are not immune should consider vaccination before pregnancy.
- The book discusses the rubella vaccine as a preventive measure.
- The author mentions that vaccination should be completed before conception.
- The text advises avoiding pregnancy for a period after vaccination.
5. Rubella antibody testing
- A blood test can determine whether a woman has antibodies against rubella.
- The author recommends checking antibody levels before pregnancy.
- Women who have previously received vaccination may already be immune.
- Some women may have immunity because of a previous infection.
- The author emphasizes that immunity testing can help women prepare for pregnancy.
6. Importance of preventing infection
- The author says that preventing infection is safer than trying to treat its effects after the baby has been exposed.
- Pregnant women should be aware of infections that can affect fetal development.
- The author encourages medical consultation when infection is suspected.
- The discussion of infectious diseases continues on the next page.
7. Rubella infection during pregnancy
- The page continues discussing infections that can affect babies during pregnancy.
- The author describes a case in which a woman became infected with rubella during the fourth or fifth month of pregnancy.
- She did not infect anyone around her and had only a mild illness.
- Although the infection appeared to be minor, the mother remained worried about her baby.
- The author explains that infections can sometimes affect the developing baby even when the mother has few symptoms.
8. Rubella immunity and the developing baby
- A mother may have antibodies against rubella because of a previous infection.
- These antibodies may provide protection against another infection.
- The author discusses how maternal immunity can affect the baby's risk of infection.
- However, the presence or absence of symptoms does not always reveal whether the fetus has been affected.
9. Chickenpox during pregnancy
- The author discusses chickenpox, an infection caused by the varicella-zoster virus.
- Women who have never had chickenpox may be vulnerable to infection during pregnancy.
- The author warns that chickenpox can sometimes affect the developing baby.
- Infection during early pregnancy may cause congenital abnormalities.
- The author says that women should be careful about exposure to chickenpox during pregnancy.
10. Chickenpox and birth defects
- The text mentions reports of babies developing abnormalities after their mothers had chickenpox.
- According to the book, approximately 2% of babies exposed to maternal chickenpox before the 20th week may develop abnormalities.
- The author explains that the risk depends on when the infection occurs.
- Some babies may be born with serious health problems.
- However, not every maternal infection results in fetal abnormalities.
11. Smallpox and pregnancy
- The author discusses smallpox, an infectious disease that was once a major health concern.
- Smallpox could cause serious complications in pregnant women.
- The author explains that infection during pregnancy could threaten the baby's life.
- The book also mentions historical cases involving smallpox and pregnancy.
12. Unusual birth defects
- The author discusses reports of babies born with unusual physical abnormalities.
- Some abnormalities may have been associated with infections during pregnancy.
- However, the author notes that these cases were uncommon.
- The text emphasizes that not every unusual birth defect can be directly linked to a particular infection.
13. Hepatitis B during pregnancy
- The author introduces hepatitis B, a viral infection that affects the liver.
- Hepatitis B can be transmitted from an infected mother to her baby.
- The author explains that some women carry the hepatitis B virus without obvious symptoms.
- A pregnant woman may appear healthy even though she has the infection.
- Therefore, blood testing is important.
14. Hepatitis B carriers
- A hepatitis B carrier is someone who has a persistent hepatitis B infection.
- The author explains that a person may carry the virus without developing noticeable liver disease.
- However, the virus can still be transmitted to other people.
- The author emphasizes the importance of identifying hepatitis B carriers during pregnancy.
15. Preventing hepatitis B transmission to babies
- The author discusses methods used to prevent hepatitis B infection in newborn babies.
- If the mother is infected, the baby may need preventive treatment immediately after birth.
- The text mentions the use of hepatitis B immune globulin.
- The author explains that this treatment provides antibodies that help protect the baby.
- The book also discusses vaccination against hepatitis B.
16. Hepatitis B testing during pregnancy
- The author mentions blood tests used to detect hepatitis B infection.
- These include tests for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).
- A positive result indicates that the person has a current hepatitis B infection.
- Further testing may be needed to understand the infection.
- The author emphasizes that pregnant women should be tested so that preventive measures can be arranged.
17. Syphilis during pregnancy
- The author introduces syphilis, a sexually transmitted bacterial infection.
- Syphilis can pass from an infected mother to her developing baby.
- The author explains that a woman may have syphilis without obvious symptoms.
- If untreated, the infection can cause serious complications during pregnancy.
- These may include miscarriage, stillbirth, or congenital syphilis.
18. Syphilis screening tests
- The author discusses blood tests used to detect syphilis.
- Tests mentioned include:
  - TPHA (Treponema pallidum hemagglutination assay).
  - FTA-ABS (Fluorescent treponemal antibody absorption test).
- The author explains that some tests may produce false-positive results.
- Therefore, additional testing may be necessary to confirm the diagnosis.
19. Treating syphilis during pregnancy
- The author emphasizes that syphilis should be diagnosed and treated.
- Early treatment can help prevent infection from reaching the baby.
- The text explains that proper medical treatment is important even when the mother has no symptoms.
- The discussion of syphilis continues on the next page.
20. Preventing sexually transmitted infections
- The page continues discussing infections that can affect pregnancy.
- The author emphasizes the importance of preventing sexually transmitted infections.
- Infections such as syphilis can harm the developing baby.
- The author advises couples to avoid sexual activities that could spread infections.
- The text recommends that husbands maintain good sexual hygiene and avoid exposing their wives to infection.

Main message of this page
The author explains how infectious diseases such as rubella, chickenpox, hepatitis B, and syphilis can affect pregnancy and the developing baby. The page emphasizes blood testing, identifying infections early, and preventing transmission from mother to child.

Important medical context: Smallpox was declared eradicated worldwide in 1980. Modern care includes routine hepatitis B and syphilis screening during pregnancy. Babies born to mothers with hepatitis B generally receive hepatitis B vaccination and hepatitis B immune globulin promptly after birth. Syphilis during pregnancy is treated with penicillin according to the infection stage.
