Chapter 14: Pregnancy in Women with Existing Medical Conditions (Continued)
1. Medicines during pregnancy
- The page continues discussing medicines used by pregnant women who have existing illnesses.
- Some medicines may affect the developing baby.
- Doctors must carefully consider which medicines are necessary during pregnancy.
- The author mentions medicines used for tuberculosis, including rifampicin and ethambutol.
- Streptomycin and pyrazinamide are described as medicines that should not be used during pregnancy, according to the book.
2. Epilepsy and pregnancy
- Epilepsy is a condition that causes repeated seizures.
- Women with epilepsy can become pregnant.
- However, some medicines used to prevent seizures may increase the risk of birth defects.
- The author explains that the risk is difficult to measure because seizures themselves and other factors may also affect pregnancy.
- The book estimates that approximately 1 in 14 babies born to women with epilepsy may have a birth defect.
3. Taking anti-seizure medicines during pregnancy
- Some anti-seizure medicines may increase the risk of congenital abnormalities.
- However, stopping epilepsy medicines suddenly can also be dangerous.
- The author recommends continuing necessary treatment rather than stopping medicines without medical advice.
- Pregnant women should have their medicine levels monitored.
- The author explains that pregnancy can change the amount of medicine circulating in the blood.
4. Adjusting epilepsy medicine doses
- The amount of medicine in the blood may decrease during pregnancy.
- Doctors may need to adjust the dosage.
- The author recommends checking blood levels approximately once a month.
- The medicine should not be stopped simply because a woman becomes pregnant.
- Proper seizure control is important for the safety of both mother and baby.
5. Thyroid disease during pregnancy
- The author discusses women with thyroid disorders.
- The thyroid gland produces hormones that control metabolism.
- Thyroid problems may affect pregnancy.
- Women with thyroid disease should receive appropriate medical treatment.
- The author explains that pregnancy can change thyroid function and treatment requirements.
6. Graves' disease (Hyperthyroidism)
- Graves' disease is a condition in which the thyroid gland produces too much thyroid hormone.
- Some women with Graves' disease become pregnant.
- The author explains that thyroid hormone levels must be carefully controlled.
- Untreated hyperthyroidism can cause complications during pregnancy.
- Doctors should monitor thyroid function regularly.
7. Treating hyperthyroidism during pregnancy
- The author discusses medicines that reduce thyroid hormone production.
- These medicines are called antithyroid drugs.
- Antithyroid medicines can pass through the placenta and affect the baby's thyroid gland.
- The author recommends using the lowest effective dose.
- Excessive treatment may reduce the baby's thyroid hormone production.
- Therefore, regular medical monitoring is important.
8. Phenylketonuria (PKU)
- Phenylketonuria, or PKU, is a genetic metabolic disorder.
- People with PKU cannot properly break down an amino acid called phenylalanine.
- Phenylalanine is found in many protein-containing foods.
- Without proper treatment, phenylalanine can build up in the blood.
- The author explains that high phenylalanine levels during pregnancy can harm the developing baby.
9. PKU and fetal development
- A woman with PKU may have no obvious symptoms if her condition is well controlled.
- However, high phenylalanine levels during pregnancy can affect the baby.
- The author mentions possible problems involving the baby's brain and heart.
- These complications may occur even when the baby does not inherit PKU.
- The mother's blood phenylalanine level is therefore very important.
10. Treating PKU before pregnancy
- The author recommends beginning a special low-phenylalanine diet before pregnancy.
- The book suggests starting treatment approximately 10 years before pregnancy, when possible.
- The author explains that controlling phenylalanine levels can reduce the risk of fetal abnormalities.
- Women with PKU should receive dietary guidance from medical professionals.
- The diet should continue throughout pregnancy.
11. High blood pressure during pregnancy
- The author discusses women who already have high blood pressure before becoming pregnant.
- High blood pressure can become more complicated during pregnancy.
- Some women may develop additional pregnancy-related complications.
- The author recommends monitoring blood pressure regularly.
- Women with high blood pressure should receive appropriate medical care.
12. What is high blood pressure?
- The book defines high blood pressure as:
  - Systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher.
  - Diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher.
- Systolic pressure is the upper number in a blood pressure reading.
- Diastolic pressure is the lower number.
- The author explains that high blood pressure can affect the health of both mother and baby.
13. Managing high blood pressure during pregnancy
- The author recommends checking blood pressure regularly.
- Women with high blood pressure may need medication.
- The author mentions that reducing salt intake was commonly recommended.
- Some women may need hospital treatment if their blood pressure becomes dangerously high.
- The goal is to protect both the mother and the developing baby.
14. Risks of high blood pressure during pregnancy
- High blood pressure can affect blood flow to the placenta.
- This may interfere with the baby's growth.
- The author warns that women with hypertension may develop pregnancy complications.
- Severe cases may require hospital admission.
- The author emphasizes the importance of medical supervision.
15. Preventing pregnancy complications
- The author recommends controlling existing medical conditions before pregnancy.
- Women should continue appropriate treatment under medical supervision.
- Regular examinations help doctors identify complications early.
- Medicines may need to be adjusted during pregnancy.

Main message of this page
The author discusses how epilepsy, thyroid disease, phenylketonuria (PKU), and high blood pressure can affect pregnancy.

The central message is that women with these conditions may have successful pregnancies, but their illnesses must be carefully controlled. Stopping prescribed medicines without medical advice can be dangerous.

Important medical context: Some recommendations in this older book are outdated. For example, streptomycin is generally avoided in pregnancy because of fetal toxicity, but pyrazinamide is used in recommended tuberculosis treatment regimens in many countries. Women with PKU should achieve good metabolic control before conception, but this does not require starting treatment 10 years beforehand. Modern management of hypertension, thyroid disease, and epilepsy during pregnancy should follow current specialist guidance.
