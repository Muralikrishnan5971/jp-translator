Chapter 14: Pregnancy in Women with Existing Medical Conditions (Continued)
1. Diabetes and pregnancy
- When a mother's blood sugar is high, the baby's pancreas responds by producing more insulin.
- This extra insulin can cause the baby to grow unusually large.
- The book explains that this can make childbirth more difficult.
- After about 28 weeks of pregnancy, the author says that women with diabetes may need hospital care.
- Around 36 weeks (9 months), doctors may consider delivering the baby early.
- A cesarean section may be performed if the baby is too large.
- After birth, the baby's blood sugar may become too low because the baby is no longer receiving glucose from the mother.
2. Treating diabetes during pregnancy
- Women who had diabetes before pregnancy may sometimes develop high blood sugar during pregnancy.
- The author recommends checking blood sugar before and after meals.
- If blood sugar is high, insulin treatment may be necessary.
- The book recommends starting insulin promptly when needed.
- If diabetes is properly controlled, the mother may be able to deliver a healthy baby.
3. Diabetes and childbirth
- The author explains that diabetes can affect both pregnancy and delivery.
- A large baby may make normal delivery difficult.
- Doctors may recommend hospital admission during the later stages of pregnancy.
- A cesarean section may be necessary in certain cases.
- After delivery, the baby's blood sugar should be monitored carefully.
4. Heart disease during pregnancy
- The author discusses women who have heart disease before becoming pregnant.
- In the past, rheumatic fever sometimes damaged the heart valves.
- Some women developed heart problems because of this damage.
- Advances in heart surgery have made pregnancy safer for many women with heart disease.
- The author explains that some women who previously underwent heart surgery can become pregnant successfully.
5. Heart surgery before pregnancy
- Women with serious heart disease may need treatment before becoming pregnant.
- The author mentions that some women undergo heart surgery before marriage or pregnancy.
- If the heart condition is successfully treated, pregnancy may become possible.
- However, not every woman with heart disease can safely become pregnant.
- Doctors must evaluate the condition of the heart before making recommendations.
6. Pregnancy after heart surgery
- The author describes women who became pregnant after heart surgery.
- Some women can carry a pregnancy safely after successful treatment.
- However, pregnancy increases the workload on the heart.
- Women with heart disease require careful medical supervision.
- The author recommends consulting a heart specialist before pregnancy.
7. Pregnancy in women with serious heart conditions
- The author explains that women with serious heart disease may face greater risks during pregnancy.
- Pregnancy increases blood volume and places additional demands on the heart.
- Women who experience heart problems should receive specialist care.
- The author emphasizes the importance of medical evaluation before pregnancy.
- Some women may be advised to delay pregnancy until their condition is treated.
8. Continuing pregnancy with heart disease
- The author says that women with heart disease should not automatically assume they cannot have children.
- Doctors should carefully assess whether pregnancy is safe.
- If the heart condition is stable, pregnancy may be possible.
- However, some serious conditions can make pregnancy dangerous.
- Medical decisions should consider both the mother's health and the baby's well-being.
9. Kidney disease and pregnancy
- The author returns to the subject of kidney disease.
- Some women have kidney abnormalities without knowing it.
- Pregnancy can reveal kidney problems that were previously unnoticed.
- Doctors should check kidney function when abnormalities are suspected.
- Women with kidney disease should receive careful monitoring during pregnancy.
10. Kidney infections during pregnancy
- The author discusses urinary tract infections and kidney infections.
- Some pregnant women develop inflammation of the kidneys.
- These infections may require medical treatment.
- The author explains that untreated infections can cause complications.
- Women with symptoms of urinary infection should consult a doctor.
11. Asymptomatic bacteriuria
- Some women have bacteria in their urine without experiencing symptoms.
- This condition is called asymptomatic bacteriuria.
- A woman may feel completely healthy even though bacteria are present in her urine.
- The author explains that these infections may become more serious during pregnancy.
- Therefore, urine testing is important.
12. Treating urinary infections
- The author recommends treating urinary infections during pregnancy.
- Antibiotics may be necessary.
- The choice of medicine should consider the safety of the developing baby.
- Even when symptoms disappear, follow-up testing may be necessary.
- The author emphasizes that infections should not be ignored.
13. Repeated urinary infections
- Some women experience urinary infections repeatedly.
- The author explains that these women may need treatment before becoming pregnant.
- Recurrent infections can sometimes affect the kidneys.
- Doctors should investigate the cause of repeated infections.
- Proper treatment may help prevent complications during pregnancy.
14. Medicines during pregnancy
- The author warns that pregnant women should be careful when taking medicines.
- Some medicines can affect the developing baby.
- The text mentions antibiotics such as:
  - Isoniazid.
  - Rifampicin.
  - Ethambutol.
- These medicines are commonly used to treat tuberculosis.
- The author explains that treatment decisions must consider both the mother's illness and the baby's safety.

Main message of this page
The author explains how diabetes, heart disease, and kidney disease can affect pregnancy.

Women with these conditions may still have healthy pregnancies, but careful medical monitoring and appropriate treatment are important.

The page also discusses urinary infections during pregnancy and the importance of choosing suitable medicines.

Important medical context: The book reflects older medical practices. Modern guidelines do not recommend routine hospital admission at 28 weeks or delivery at 36 weeks for every woman with diabetes. The timing of delivery depends on blood sugar control, fetal growth, and other complications. Similarly, tuberculosis medicines should not be stopped simply because of pregnancy; treatment should be supervised by a qualified doctor.
