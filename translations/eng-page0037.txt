Chapter 4: Hereditary Diseases (Continued)
1. Hereditary diseases and genetic counselling
- Some diseases, such as certain muscle disorders, can be passed from parents to children through genes.
- People who have hereditary diseases often know that their condition runs in their family.
- If someone has a hereditary disease, they may worry about passing it to their children.
- The author advises people to seek genetic counselling rather than becoming overly pessimistic.
- Genetic counselling can help people understand the chances of passing a disease to their children.
- However, the author warns that genetic counselling cannot always provide a definite answer.
2. Understanding family medical history
- A genetic counsellor may prepare a family tree to study hereditary diseases.
- The counsellor examines whether parents, grandparents, or other relatives had similar diseases.
- Even if a disease appeared two or three generations earlier, it may still be relevant.
- However, hereditary diseases do not always follow simple inheritance patterns.
- A person can carry a disease-causing gene without showing symptoms.
3. Not all diseases are inherited
- The author explains that many diseases are caused by factors other than heredity.
- Environmental conditions and other influences can contribute to diseases.
- Parents may have a disease without necessarily passing it to their children.
- A mother may have a hereditary condition while her daughter remains healthy.
- The author gives examples of conditions such as:
  - Blood disorders.
  - Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
  - Phenylketonuria (PKU).
- These diseases have different patterns of inheritance.
- The author says that parents should not automatically assume their children will inherit their illnesses.
4. Genetic risks during pregnancy
- Even when both parents appear healthy, their baby may sometimes develop a genetic disorder.
- The author discusses the possibility of detecting abnormalities before birth.
- Some birth defects and genetic disorders can be identified through prenatal testing.
- However, the author explains that not every genetic condition can be detected during pregnancy.
5. Causes of miscarriage
- The author states that some miscarriages happen because of abnormalities in the developing baby.
- The text provides the following figures for causes of miscarriage:

- These figures are presented in the book and should not be interpreted as established modern statistics.
- The author discusses how abnormalities in fetal development may contribute to pregnancy complications.
6. Amniocentesis and prenatal testing
- Amniocentesis is a medical procedure used to examine the health of a developing baby.
- During this procedure, a small amount of amniotic fluid is collected from the uterus.
- The fluid contains cells from the developing baby.
- Doctors can examine these cells to look for certain genetic or chromosomal abnormalities.
- The author says the procedure was generally not performed before the 16th week of pregnancy.
- The text warns that prenatal testing may involve risks.
- It also mentions that results may take several weeks.
7. Detecting abnormalities before birth
- The author describes how doctors can study fetal cells to identify certain genetic conditions.
- The procedure may help identify chromosome abnormalities and some inherited diseases.
- However, it cannot detect every disease or disability.
- The author also mentions examining fetal blood or umbilical cord blood to detect abnormalities.
- Such tests may help doctors identify certain blood disorders before birth.
8. Diseases that prenatal testing may detect
The author mentions several conditions that may be identified through prenatal testing, depending on the test used:
- Down syndrome: A genetic condition usually caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21.
- Spina bifida: A birth defect affecting the development of the spine.
- Phenylketonuria (PKU): An inherited metabolic disorder.
- Blood disorders: Certain inherited conditions affecting the blood.
- Congenital infections: Infections that can affect a baby before birth, including rubella and cytomegalovirus.
9. Parents and responsibility
- The author argues that parents should understand the possibility of hereditary diseases before having children.
- Families should consider their medical history when planning a pregnancy.
- The author suggests that genetic counselling can help parents make informed decisions.
- However, the text also acknowledges that genetic testing cannot predict every possible outcome.
- The author states that parents have responsibilities toward their children, but also emphasizes that some conditions cannot be predicted or prevented.
